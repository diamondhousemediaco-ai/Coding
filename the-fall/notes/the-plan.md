# THE FALL — THE PLAN

Everything we've built, assembled into one movie, in order. **This is a decisive pass —
I made the calls instead of listing options.** Anything you hate, we change; the point is
to have a spine solid enough to argue with.

Placeholder name in use: the barista is **MAGGIE** *(rename freely)*.

---

## THE MOVIE IN ONE PARAGRAPH

Bill is fifty, invisible, catastrophically unlucky and deeply kind. He wants to be
somebody so badly he'll try anything. A humiliating one-wheel wipeout goes viral and
hands him everything he ever asked for — money, fame, strangers screaming his name
(the wrong name). He leases a millionaire's life, burns every cent chasing the feeling,
and loses it all, including the woman who's known him since they were kids. At the
bottom, broke and living with his mother, he does the first honest thing in the movie:
he blows up his own last chance to expose the man who's been stealing the pensions from
the company he gave nineteen years to — including the pension of the one friend who told
him to save his money. Nobody films it. **And that's the thing that finally gets him
seen.**

# ACT ONE — THE WANT
*~28 pages. Broad, fast, funny.*

### 1. Cold open — the coffee shop
Bill walks in. **Maggie is already making his drink.** They talk like two people who
have known each other since they were nine — because they have. Coffee goes down his
shirt. She slides napkins across without looking up.

**Narration begins** *(Free Guy register — he's confiding while we see the truth)*:
who he is, what he wants, *"nothing ever goes my way."* He tells us about Maggie —
**childhood friends, thirty years, he's been about to say something for most of them.**

> The audience gets the whole relationship free, in ninety seconds, before the plot
> starts. This is also the last scene of the movie.

### 2. The scheme montage
Narrated, fast, escalating:
- **The stock.** Dumps his $10,000 life savings. Line goes up, then craters. Four
  seconds and a shrug. ⚠️ *Shot-for-shot setup for the crash out — same app, same
  framing, same sound.*
- **The lottery.** He lets an older lady go ahead of him. She buys his tickets. **She
  wins, big, right there at the counter.** He's genuinely happy for her. She never
  realizes he let her go. ⚠️ *His bad luck is caused by his own decency. This is why we
  love him.*
- **The MLM.** Hotel ballroom seminar. Forty boxes arrive. ⚠️ *Runner — those boxes
  never leave.*
- **Failed viral attempts.** He tried to go viral on purpose. Dances, trends, "day in
  my life" with nothing in it. Thirty-one views.
- **The drug deal.** Hoodie, walks to a car, gets the badge flipped on him, arrested on
  the hood. *"Yeah. Tried that."* **CALL: what he's selling isn't actually a drug** —
  the cop lets him go, disgusted. No felony to explain later.
- **The rear-end scam.** He brake-checks, gets hit — **and is so thrilled it worked he
  leaps out celebrating.** On the dashcam. Claim dead.
- **Selling life insurance** door to door. Wrecks a living room.

### 3. The office
Nineteen years. Still an associate. **The boss drops his own work on Bill's desk** and
leaves. ⚠️ *The stack. Plant it flat, no comment.*
**The company photo on the wall — Bill's face is completely hidden behind someone's
head.** ⚠️ *Known vs. seen, in a prop. Final shot of the movie pays it.*

### 4. The kids
Gaming. He's last alive and dies to **fall damage.** They drop him instantly. *"Those
are honestly some of the closest friends I've had in a while."* They troll him about
**"your coffee girl."**

### 5. Mom
She calls. She's proud of him. **She got him that job twenty years ago** and brings it
up. He's embarrassed by her and gets off the phone.

### 6. The date
It's going **well.** She likes him. He's being himself and it's working — and he can't
leave it alone. *"Can I ask you something? Do you have anybody who depends on you
financially?"* She's gone. ⚠️ *Not clumsiness. Reaching. Same move he makes twice more.*

### 7. THE FALL
Downtown Nashville. One-wheel. He's happy for once. Two twins in flat-bills:
*"Yo — look at this guy on the one-wheel vibing. This is Nashville baby. Record it."*
He goes down. *"OHHHHH."* — *"No way you got that bro."* — *"Should I post it? I kinda
feel bad."* — **"Definitely post it."**

**Thumb hits post. END ACT ONE.**

# ACT TWO-A — THE HAVE
*~28 pages. Funniest stretch of the film, with the trap closing underneath.*

### 8. The office, next morning
The video is already viral. A colleague plays it out loud. They pile on. He leaves early.

### 9. Home, alone — THE DING
Depressed on the couch. Then: **ding. ding-ding. ding.** Notifications stacking.
**Music up. He dances. Alone. Ecstatic.**

> ⚠️ **THESIS SHOT OF THE MOVIE.** He is alone in a room celebrating being watched.
> Shoot it deliberately — the crash out reuses this exact setup, reversed.
> This is where he becomes an addict. Humiliation first, then the hit.

### 10. The next morning — swagger
Strangers on the street: *"You're the guy!"* Selfies. He eats it up.
**Into the coffee shop, strutting.** Maggie watches him. She doesn't say anything.
⚠️ *First crack. The audience feels a drop; Bill is too high to.*

### 11. The desk, one more time
He sits down. **The boss drops the stack of paper on him.** Nothing here cares that
he's famous. *I have to get out of here.*

### 12. The agent calls
Enter **the agent** — warm, funny, genuinely likes Bill, and needs him to stay a
spectacle. Never lies. Never cruel. ⚠️ *Your antagonist.*

### 13. The commercial montage
**Fell** helmets. **Neokay** — *"Make your knee OK with Neokay."* And the big one:
**the Life Alert parody — "Have you fallen and can't get up?"** A man desperate to be
somebody becomes the national face of a device for people who can't get off the floor.
He takes it, because it pays. ⚠️ *He's advertising help arriving after a fall. Nobody
comes when he goes down.*

### 14. "I QUIT"
Cars first. He pulls up outside the office, **lays on the horn until the boss appears at
the window**, shreds the street with donuts and burnouts, and leaves tire marks reading
**"I QUIT."** Peels off. Never says a word.
- ⚠️ **Point of no return.** Income is now one-time. Payments are forever.
- ⚠️ **Mom is the only person not celebrating.** He burned what she gave him, laughing.
- Small scrape on the way out, at the edge of frame.

### 15. The signing montage
Mansion, cars, yacht, cards. **He leases and finances all of it, signing without reading
anything.** ⚠️ *Fixes the math. Creates the clock. States the theme: he's renting a life.*

### 16. The rise
Parties, cannonball into his own pool, a fifty-year-old living a rich teenager's
fantasy. **Peak comedy — spend it all here.**

### 17. The gold digger
The agent puts it in his ear: with this money he can have any woman he wants.
**He loves that idea**, and she's exactly what he ordered. She's working him and he
**cannot see it — because he doesn't want to.** Seeing her clearly means admitting the
money didn't make him lovable.
⚠️ *Tells for the audience only: affectionate near cameras, flat when not; a different
voice on the phone; specific about objects, vague about him; never asks him a question
about himself.*

### 18. "The face on three"
Strangers making him recreate his falling face. Funny, then grating, then humiliating.

## MIDPOINT — THE "BOB" PARTY
*~pg 55–60. The tone turns here and never fully comes back.*

### 19. Maggie's warning
She tells him what the gold digger is. **He calls it jealousy** and throws it back at
her. **She crashes out.** Twenty years of not saying it, and this is what comes out.

### 20. The party
It's a **brand-sponsored one-year-anniversary-of-the-fall party** that doubles as his
birthday. Which is why it's enormous, why there's a hired rapper, and why the room is
full of strangers.

**"Big happy birthday to our guy — BOB!"**

A thousand people scream a name that isn't his. Total adoration. Total isolation.

# ACT TWO-B — THE LOSE
*~28 pages. The laughs get thinner on purpose.*

### 21. The comedown
Parties bigger, Bill smaller. The dings are constant and oppressive now.

### 22. He stops calling his mom
Phone buzzes at a party. **"Mom."** Declined. ⚠️ *The most damning small thing in the
movie. One shot.*

### 23. The money slows
Deals don't renew. A newer, younger viral guy is taking meetings. The agent takes longer
to call back.

### 24. The spending doesn't
Gold digger pushes, he offers. He's buying what Maggie gave him for free.

### 25. THE DECLINED CARD
Designer boutique — **the same store where they fawned over him in Act Two.** Her card
declines. **The mask comes off in one beat.** She's done, instantly. Gone.
**Cut to his phone lighting up somewhere else.** He learns he's broke and learns he's
alone in the same instant.

### 26. The repossessions
Cars taken **one at a time.** Eviction from the mansion. Nothing sold — everything
**taken**, because none of it was his.

### 27. THE SILENCE
**The phone stops.** No dings. An absence where a sound has been for an hour of screen
time. The last notification he gets is spam.

### 28. He does the math
He never owned anything. **He rented a life for a year and gave it back.**

### 29. THE CRASH OUT
Last **$10,000** — the same number he started with. Into a stock. **It crashes, in the
identical shot from the opening montage** — but with the narration, the music and the
cutaway stripped out. The camera does not leave him.

He reaches for *"nothing ever goes my way"* — **and it doesn't work.** He can't make it
funny anymore.

**Full breakdown. No audience. Nobody films it. Nobody to call.**

### 30. Home
Fifty years old, moving back in with his mother. **The MLM boxes are still in the
hallway.** She doesn't say I told you so. Not once.

# ACT THREE — THE TRUTH
*~25 pages. He earns this act.*

### 31. Bankruptcy court
The judge. Everything on the record. ⚠️ *Remember this room and this judge.*

### 32. He goes back to beg
To the office. For his old desk. The one he burned rubber quitting.

### 33. He sees it
**The boss's fraud.** And he's the only person alive who could have spotted it — because
he's the only one who ever actually did the work. ⚠️ **Nineteen years of being invisible
is exactly what makes him the only possible witness.**

### 34. The choice
Say nothing and get his job back. Or blow it up and lose the last thing he has.

### 35. ⭐ THE BOARDROOM — HIS RACE
He blows it up. **And he's terrible at it** — sweating, dropping pages, losing his
place, the projector fights him. **He carries in the stack**, nineteen years of the
boss's work in Bill's own handwriting.

And he is completely, devastatingly right.

> ⚠️ **THIS IS THE WIN.** For the first time in the movie he does something that costs
> him everything and gains him nothing. **No camera. No crowd. No upside.** He performed
> for millions and got nothing real; he does one honest thing for nobody, and that's what
> finally gets him seen.

He walks out with no job, and it's the best he's ever felt.

### 36. He goes to her
**He goes to Maggie. She does not rescue him.** He walks in with nothing and asks.

### 37. The rebuild
They go out. **The carnival** — he can't win her a prize, gets furious at himself, and
**she lights up.** The exact thing the world laughed at is the thing she's loved for
thirty years.

### 38. The courthouse wedding
**Same courtroom. Same judge.** Tiny. Almost nobody. **Mom is there.** She says his
real name and means it — the payoff of "Bob."

⚠️ **He marries her broke.** Non-negotiable. It's the only way the movie proves she
wasn't bought.

### 39. The bike
Small apartment. **She buys him a bike** — two wheels, a quiet joke about looking after
him. He gets on it and immediately eats it. **She loses it laughing. And he laughs
too** — the first time in the movie he laughs at his own fall.

**Nobody films it.**

### 40. TWO YEARS LATER
Small life. Modest job. Happy. **Then corporate comes back for him.**
*"Well — looks like we're looking at our new CEO."*

⚠️ **This lands AFTER he's already content, which is the whole reason it doesn't
undercut the theme.** It isn't what made him happy; it's the world catching up. It's
**recognition, not reward** — the first time anyone looked at Bill and saw what was
actually there.

### 41. He races home
She's **thirty weeks pregnant.** He tells her. **He picks her up and kisses her.**

### 42. FINAL IMAGE
**The new company photo on the wall.** And this time you can see his face.

# THE CALLS I MADE

1. **The order of the ending.** Boardroom → he wins her → **married broke** → the bike →
   *then* two years later, the CEO job, then the baby. Getting the promotion **after**
   he's already happy is what stops it from being a status prize.
2. **The boardroom is the climax**, not the promotion. He acts. That fixes the passivity
   problem that ran through the whole story.
3. **The drug isn't a real drug** — no felony to explain away later.
4. **The birthday party and the anniversary party are one event.** It explains the scale,
   the rapper, and why nobody knows his name.
5. **The agent is the antagonist.** Friendly, honest, and dependent on Bill staying a
   spectacle.
6. **The barista is called Maggie** until you say otherwise.

# MAJOR ADDITION — CLYDE, AND THE STOLEN PENSIONS

*Added after the first full pass. This changes Act Three fundamentally and
retroactively charges most of Act One.*

## Who he is

**CLYDE** *(placeholder name)* — an older Black gentleman who works with Bill.
He is **the voice of reason.** He's been telling Bill to save his money and be
smart with it for years, and Bill has never once listened.

**He's retiring in a couple of months.**

## What happens

The boss's fraud isn't abstract. **He's been stealing the pensions.** When it
comes apart, **everybody gets laid off**, and the retirement money everyone spent
their working lives building is gone.

Including Clyde's. Weeks before he was supposed to be free.

---

## ⚠️ WHY THIS IS THE BIGGEST FIX IN THE DOCUMENT

### 1. It gives Bill something to fight FOR

Before this, the boardroom climax was Bill doing an honest thing on principle,
and the only thing at risk was his own job. Honorable, but thin.

Now the fraud has **victims with faces.** Bill isn't defending an abstraction —
he's fighting for a man who spent forty years doing everything right. That's the
*Free Guy* "saving the island" beat the movie was missing.

### 2. It fixes the CEO ending completely

The old problem: the movie spends two hours proving status is empty, then hands
Bill the biggest status prize in the film. Even reframed as recognition, it
scraped against the theme.

**Now the promotion isn't a reward. It's the tool.**

He becomes CEO because it's **the only position from which he can put the pension
money back.** He doesn't want the title, he needs the authority. That's not a man
being handed a prize — that's a man taking a job because there's work to do.

### 3. Clyde is the anti-Bill, and the movie gets brutal about it

- Bill wants everything now. Clyde saved patiently for forty years
- **Bill blows a fortune and gets his life back. Clyde does everything right and
  loses his.**
- That's the world's unfairness stated as character, and it's the deepest possible
  reading of *"nothing ever goes my way"* — it turns out that was never really
  about Bill

### 4. It retroactively charges Act One

Every scene where Clyde says *"save your money"* and Bill waves him off becomes a
plant. Nothing needs to be added — those scenes just start meaning something.

### 5. It answers an open question

**"What is the boss's fraud?"** — the pensions. Concrete, comprehensible in one
line, and genuinely devastating.

---

## ⚠️ CRAFT WARNING — DO NOT LET CLYDE BE A MARTYR

The wise older Black mentor who suffers so the white lead can grow is a
well-worn and badly-regarded trope. The story is one small decision away from it
and one small decision away from avoiding it entirely.

**The fix: Clyde noticed first.**

He's been suspicious for months. He tried to raise it and **nobody listened to
him either** — he's the other invisible man in that building. He isn't rescued by
Bill; **they do it together**, and each brings the half the other lacks:

- **Clyde** has been watching, and knows something is wrong
- **Bill** has nineteen years of doing the boss's actual work, so he's the only
  one who can prove *what* is wrong

**Two invisible men who between them know everything.** Clyde should be in the
boardroom. He should act, not be avenged.

He also needs a life outside this: what he was going to do with that retirement,
who's waiting for him, what he wanted.

# ADDITION — MEMES, AND HOW TO USE THEM WITHOUT DATING THE MOVIE

## The hazard, stated plainly

A film takes two to four years from script to screen. **A meme that's enormous
today will be dead and embarrassing by release**, and dated references age a
movie faster than anything else in it. Half the comedies of any given decade are
unwatchable for exactly this reason.

That's not a reason to skip it. It's a reason to be deliberate.

---

## ⚠️ THE BIG IDEA — BILL *IS* THE MEME. USE THE MEME CYCLE AS PLOT.

Don't decorate the movie with memes. **Make the meme lifecycle the shape of his
fame**, because that's literally what the story is about. His clip should move
through the whole cycle on screen:

1. **The raw clip.** Just the fall.
2. **Edited to music.** Somebody sets it to a song. It gets bigger than the original.
3. **A reaction image.** One frame of his face, captioned about something else.
4. **A sound.** People use the audio of him hitting the ground. The image is gone.
5. **A template.** The format has nothing to do with him anymore.
6. **Dead.** Nobody posts it. That's the silence.

**That is his entire arc, told in a language the audience already speaks, without
a line of dialogue.**

### ⚠️ The devastating beat this hands you

Late in the movie, **Bill sees his own fall used as a meme by someone who has no
idea it's a real person** — a stranger's phone, a stranger's post, his face
detached from him entirely, being used to mean something completely unrelated.

**That's the "Bob" moment in visual form.** His face is famous and it isn't his
anymore. Put it near the comedown or right before the crash out.

---

## THE FOUR RULES FOR ACTUALLY DOING IT

**1. Invent anything load-bearing.**
If a meme has to carry a scene, make it up. Then it can't date you, you control
it completely, and — better — it can be **about Bill**, which is what you want
anyway.

**2. Put real memes only in the kids' mouths.**
Coming from the gaming kids and the twins, a current reference reads as
*characterization* — kids talk like this. If it's stale by release, it's stale in
a way that still tells you who they are. Coming from the movie itself, it reads
as the film trying to be cool and failing.

> **The model is already in the script:** *"You have no aura."* That's exactly
> right, because a kid says it to a fifty-year-old who doesn't understand it.
> The joke is the gap, not the reference.

**3. Use meme *grammar*, not meme *specifics*.**
The structure of how something spreads — the edit, the sound, the caption, the
remix, the ironic repost — is durable. The specific format is not.

**4. Easter eggs stay in the background, where they can be replaced.**
Comment sections, phone screens, a t-shirt, a party banner, background TV.
Nothing important depends on the audience catching them, and **anything shot as
background can be swapped late** if something better exists by the time you make
it.

---

## WHERE THEY GO

- **The gaming kids' voice chat** — their natural habitat, best density
- **The twins**, before and after the fall
- **Comment sections** — pause-and-read gags, freeze-frame Easter eggs
- **"The face on three"** — this is already a meme behavior, it just isn't
  labelled as one. Strangers performing his format at him
- **The brand commercials** — a corporate ad clumsily using a dead meme is
  eternally funny and ages *well*, because being out of touch is the joke
- **The party** — banners and decorations built on his own meme, which he now
  hates

---

## THE TEST

> **If the joke needs the audience to know the meme, it will break.**
> **If the joke is that Bill doesn't know it, it will last.**

He's fifty. Everything about this world is already foreign to him. Point the
comedy at that gap and it stays funny in 2035.

# ADDITION — THE KIDS: VENTING, AND THE HANGOUT

## A) Bill vents to the kids about his boss

### ⚠️ PLACEMENT: **AFTER THE MONEY, BEFORE HE QUITS.**

Not Act One. This sits in the narrow window where **the brand-deal money is
already coming in and he is still sitting at that desk taking abuse.** Roughly
between the agent's call and the burnout.

That window is the whole point of the scene. He doesn't have to be there
anymore — and he still is, because nineteen years of being nobody doesn't
evaporate the second a check clears.

**On the headset.** Bill is complaining about his boss, his job, his life — to a
squad of twelve-year-olds. They give him advice with total confidence, and it is
terrible.

*"Bro. Show him who's boss."*

The comedy is the mismatch: a man in his fifties, newly rich, taking career
counsel from children who cannot drive. Play his side completely sincere —
**he's actually listening.** That's what makes it funny and what makes it sad.

### ⚠️ THIS IS A PLANT, AND MOVING IT LATE MAKES IT BETTER

**The "I QUIT" burnout is a twelve-year-old's idea of quitting a job — because a
twelve-year-old gave it to him, minutes earlier.**

He takes their advice. Literally, and immediately. That's why he doesn't hand in
a notice, doesn't have a conversation, doesn't do anything an adult would do: he
gets the boss to the window and burns rubber, **because that's what the kids told
him to do.**

Setting it up right before the payoff instead of forty pages earlier means the

## E) The kids help him pull it off in Act Three

**They're on his side. Make them useful.**

Bill's evidence is nineteen years of paper — the stack. What he needs is it
organized, digitized, searchable, and put into something he can show a boardroom.
**He has no idea how to do any of that. They can do it in an afternoon.**

### ⚠️ THE JOKE THIS UNLOCKS

**The boardroom presentation was made by twelve-year-olds.**

That's why the slides are strange. That's why the projector fights him. That's
why there's a transition nobody has used since 2009 and a font that shouldn't
exist. **And it's why he has any presentation at all.**

It gives the climax comedy without weakening it — he's still right, he's still
brave, and the room still has to listen. The material is just visibly the work of
children who were extremely excited to help.

### Plant it in the hangout scene

of them casually does something technical Bill can't — fixes his TV, sets

### What they might actually do

- Build the deck
- Find something public that corroborates it
- Screen-record something before it disappears
- One of them knows something about the software the company uses, because of

organize what Bill already lawfully has. That keeps it funny and keeps him

# MAJOR DEVELOPMENT — THE BANK, AND THE FRAME-UP

*This supersedes the earlier "insurance back office" option and refines the
pension idea into one cleaner mechanism.*

## The setup

**Bill works at a bank.** He's in a department downstairs with **Clyde** and the
rest of his coworkers. **The antagonist runs the top floor.**

**The boss is stealing.** And to cover the shortfall, he's making it look like
**Bill's floor is the one underperforming.** The money isn't missing — on paper,
a department simply isn't producing what it should.

So he takes it to corporate: *that department isn't carrying its weight.*
**Lay them off.**

## ⚠️ WHY HE PICKED THAT FLOOR — THIS IS THE WHOLE MOVIE

**He chose Bill's department precisely because nobody there matters.**

Overlooked people are the safest people to frame. Nobody checks their numbers,
nobody asks them questions, nobody would believe them over him. **The villain's
crime is an expression of the exact thing the film is about** — he weaponized
their invisibility, the same invisibility that has defined Bill's entire life.

Say it once, quietly, and never again.

## What it fixes

- **Group stakes.** It isn't only Clyde now — it's the whole floor, everyone Bill
  spent nineteen years beside
- **Clyde's pension has one clean mechanism.** He doesn't get robbed in a separate
  scheme — **he gets laid off weeks before he retires**, and the retirement goes
  with the job. Simpler and more brutal
- **Act Three becomes a mission with a goal**, not a moral position: *get the
  proof*
- **The location list already fits** — "Studio Bank" was on your scout

---

# ⚠️ HOW HE GETS IN — DO NOT MAKE THIS A HEIST

The instinct to have him get onto the top floor is right. The execution is where
this could go wrong: the second it becomes a caper — vents, timers, a crew, a
plan — it's a different movie and it will strangle the emotional ending.

**The answer is already in your story:**

> ### HIS INVISIBILITY IS HOW HE GETS IN.
>
> He doesn't outsmart anybody. He doesn't sneak. **He walks onto that floor and
> nobody looks at him**, because nobody ever has. Nineteen years of being furniture,
> and it turns out furniture goes anywhere.
>
> Maybe his badge still works because nobody bothered to deactivate a nobody.
> Maybe someone holds the door. Maybe he says he's there to drop something off and
> that's the end of the conversation.

**The thing that ruined his life is the thing that wins.** That's the movie
paying off its own thesis with no action beats, no cleverness, and no tonal
break. It should be almost unbearably easy — and a little sad that it is.

---

# ⚠️ THE REALIZATION — MAKE IT HANDWRITING

You asked for the moment Bill *knows*. Play it quiet, and make it physical rather
than technical, so the audience gets it instantly:

**Bill did all of the boss's work for nineteen years. So every real figure in
that building is in Bill's handwriting.**

**The only numbers that aren't in his hand are the ones the boss wrote himself.**

That's the proof. He doesn't need to be an accountant or a hacker. He just needs
to be the one man alive who can look at a page and say: *I didn't write that. And
I wrote everything.*

- It's visual, it's instant, and an audience understands it with no exposition
- **It's THE STACK again** — the same stack the boss dropped on his desk in Act
  One, the same stack he carries into the boardroom
- It makes his nineteen wasted years the literal instrument of the win

**Where he realizes it:** ideally not on the top floor. He should figure it out
somewhere ordinary — his mother's kitchen table, the coffee shop — and *then* go
get what he needs. Realization and action as two separate moves.

---

# KEEP IT LEAN

The film is a character comedy, not a procedural. Corporate-crime plotting will
eat pages fast. **Three moves, no more:**

1. **He realizes** (handwriting)
2. **He gets it** (walks in unseen)
3. **He shows it** (the boardroom, with Clyde, with the kids' terrible deck)

Anything beyond those three is a subplot the movie doesn't have room for.
